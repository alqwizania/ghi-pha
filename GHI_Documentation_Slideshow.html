The problem

Outbreaks are reported before they are confirmed

The first signal of an emerging event is almost never a laboratory result. It is a news report, a ministry statement, or a physician noticing something unusual — arriving scattered across dozens of agencies, in several languages, at all hours.

Before

Manual scanning

An analyst opens the same thirty websites each morning. Coverage depends on who is on shift, how much time they have, and which languages they read.

The gap

Volume beats attention

There is no shortage of health news. There is a shortage of judgement about which item, out of hundreds, will matter to the Kingdom next month.

What GHI does

Collect, score, escalate

Machines do the reading and the arithmetic. People do the judgement. Everything the system decides is recorded with the reasoning that produced it.

This is the model WHO calls Event-Based Surveillance — the same approach behind WHO's own EIOS platform. GHI applies it with Saudi priorities built into the scoring.

How it works

Two streams in, one decision out

Every item follows the same path and no step is skipped. Verification comes before risk assessment, exactly as WHO's event-based surveillance requires — a rumour cannot escalate itself.

01 · COLLECT

Global Radar

59 official sources checked automatically. A page is only re-read when its content actually changes.

02 · COLLECT

Social Listener

12 accounts on X — ministries, WHO, ProMED, regional experts — matched against 64 watch terms.

03 · SCORE

IHR Scoring

Scored against the four IHR Annex 2 questions. Anything clearing the threshold reaches an analyst.

04 · DECIDE

Triage

An analyst accepts or rejects. Acceptance opens an assessment; rejection is recorded with a reason.

05 · ASSESS

Assessment

IHR Annex 2 and a Rapid Risk Assessment, pre-drafted by the system, edited by the analyst.

06 · ACT

Escalation

Director review with the full reasoning attached — which questions were answered yes, and why.

Automated

Steps 3 and 5 run without a person. Every automated output is a draft. The analyst's answer is stored separately and always takes precedence — the machine's original position stays on the record, so any disagreement is visible and auditable.

The method

Scored against international law, not intuition

Priority is not a feeling. Every signal is scored on the four questions the International Health Regulations (2005) Annex 2 requires a State Party to ask.

Question 1

Serious impact?

Judged against the expected level for that disease in that country — not against raw case counts.

Question 2

Unusual or unexpected?

Novelty is the strongest single predictor of a public health emergency of international concern.

Question 3

Risk of spread?

Healthcare-worker infection weighs heavily — the classic sentinel for sustained human transmission.

Question 4

Travel or trade risk?

Border measures and advisories carry direct economic consequence for the Kingdom.

The escalation rule

Any two of four means notify WHO.
That is Annex 2's own threshold, not a number this system invented. A high score is therefore a prediction of the legal outcome, not a separate opinion about it.

Anomaly, not magnitude

Forty cholera cases in Yemen during an ongoing epidemic is background. Four in Riyadh is an emergency.
45 disease baselines encode what “expected” means per disease and country, so severity is measured as deviation.

Why preprints sit below Reuters

A preprint has not been peer reviewed. Treating it as “scientific, therefore reliable” is how a paper that is later withdrawn becomes a national alert.

Analyst experience

The assessment is written before the analyst opens it

Accepting a signal produces a completed IHR Annex 2 answer set and Rapid Risk Assessment, with the reasoning behind each answer. The analyst edits rather than composes.

Example — generated, unedited

MERS-CoV — Saudi Arabia

“Observed CFR 39.0% is at or above the 35% baseline. Respiratory transmission route. MERS-CoV is endemic in Saudi Arabia; occurrence is expected rather than novel.”

The analyst always wins

The draft is frozen the moment it is written. Analyst edits are stored separately, so the difference between the two is the override record — no precedence rule, nothing to configure, and the machine's original position remains available for audit.

No model decides risk

Language models read facts out of source pages — disease, place, counts, dates. Every risk classification is deterministic code. A health authority must be able to explain why something was rated Critical, and “the model judged it so” is not an explanation that survives review.

Uncertainty is stated, not hidden

Each draft lists what is missing — no case counts, no baseline on record, a single uncorroborated source. Those are precisely the things overlooked under load.
